What Are Greenhouse Gases?

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) are gases in the atmosphere that trap heat. They allow short‑wave radiation (sunlight) to pass through the atmosphere but absorb long‑wave infrared radiation that the Earth re‑emits. This keeps the planet warm enough for life.

Main greenhouse gases (GCSE):

  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): Released from burning fossil fuels and deforestation.
  • Methane (CH₄): Produced by cattle, rice fields, and landfill sites.
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O): Released from fertilisers and industry.
  • Water vapour (H₂O): Naturally present; increases as temperatures rise.

Why Are Greenhouse Gases Important?

Greenhouse gases are essential for maintaining a stable climate. Without them, Earth would be around 33°C colder and unable to support most life.

They help by:

  • Keeping Earth warm enough for liquid water.
  • Maintaining stable climates and ecosystems.
  • Allowing agriculture and biodiversity to exist.

How Human Activities Increase Greenhouse Gases

Since the Industrial Revolution, humans have increased greenhouse gas levels dramatically. This strengthens the natural greenhouse effect, causing the enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming.

Main human sources:

  • Burning fossil fuels: Cars, power stations, heating → CO₂.
  • Deforestation: Fewer trees to absorb CO₂.
  • Agriculture: Cattle produce methane; fertilisers release N₂O.
  • Landfill sites: Decomposing waste releases methane.

The Greenhouse Effect (GCSE Explanation)

The greenhouse effect is a natural process. The Sun emits short‑wave radiation that passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth. The Earth then emits long‑wave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb this heat and re‑radiate it in all directions.

Human activity increases greenhouse gas levels, making the insulating layer thicker. This is the enhanced greenhouse effect, which leads to global warming.

Consequences of Global Warming

Global warming causes major environmental and biological impacts:

  • Rising sea levels: Melting ice caps + thermal expansion.
  • Loss of biodiversity: Species unable to adapt quickly enough.
  • More extreme weather: Storms, droughts, heatwaves.
  • Changes in migration patterns: Birds, insects, and fish shift ranges.
  • Coral bleaching: Warmer oceans stress coral reefs.
  • Food insecurity: Crop failures in some regions.

Other Gases Released by Burning Fossil Fuels

Burning fossil fuels also releases harmful pollutants that are not greenhouse gases but still damage health and the environment.

  • Sulfur dioxide (SO₂): Causes acid rain; damages plants and buildings.
  • Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ): Contribute to smog and acid rain; irritate lungs.
  • Particulates (PM2.5/PM10): Tiny particles that cause respiratory and heart problems.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO): Toxic gas that reduces oxygen transport in blood.
  • Unburnt hydrocarbons: Contribute to ground‑level ozone formation.
